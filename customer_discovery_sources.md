# Island Tales — Customer Discovery Source Notes

## Source 1 — Independent Publishers Guild / Anansesem
URL: https://independentpublishersguild.com/IPG/IPG/Latest/Blogs/IPG/Posts/The-importance-of-Caribbean-children-s-literature.aspx

Evidence type: First-person publisher/platform account reproduced by the Independent Publishers Guild.

Key findings:
- Anansesem was founded in 2010 to fill a gap in children's and YA books and described itself as the only English-language publication devoted to Caribbean children's and YA literature at the time.
- It publishes, reviews, and highlights Caribbean writers and illustrators and acts as a forum for publishing discussions.
- The article says there is a strong need to discover and publish more contemporary Caribbean writing for children and young adults.
- Reported barriers include a shortage of literary agents willing to represent authentically Caribbean stories, limited resources for cover design, and insufficient professional-development opportunities for writers based in the Caribbean.
- It states that few physical libraries and bookstores stock Caribbean children's books, contributing to low public awareness of the breadth of the literature.

Useful direct quotes:
> "We founded Anansesem in 2010 to fill a big gap in children’s and young adult (YA) books."

> "There is a strong need to discover and publish more contemporaneous Caribbean writing for children and young adults in the UK."

> "There are many challenges, like a shortage of literary agents willing to represent authentically Caribbean stories, a lack of resources for things like cover design, and insufficient professional development opportunities for writers based in the Caribbean."

> "Few bricks-and-mortar libraries and bookstores stock these books... Unsurprisingly, many people aren’t yet aware of the full breadth, depth and vibrancy of this body of literature."

Implication: Strong evidence for an author-side discoverability/distribution problem and a reader-side access/awareness problem. This directly supports testing a Caribbean story discovery and distribution platform, but the source is historical and should be triangulated with more recent evidence.

## Source 2 — UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Latin America and the Caribbean
URL: https://gem-report-2020.unesco.org/latin-america-and-the-caribbean/

Evidence type: Regional education monitoring report.

Key findings:
- The report describes Latin America and the Caribbean as the world's most unequal region and says school systems mirror that inequality.
- Attendance is lower for some marginalized groups, including young people with disabilities, indigenous-language speakers, and Afro-descendants; the report specifically references Trinidad and Tobago among countries with disability-related attendance gaps.
- One in two Latin American 15-year-olds does not achieve minimum reading proficiency.
- In the Dominican Republic, Guatemala, and Panama, fewer than 20 of the poorest 15-year-old students for every 100 of their richest peers achieve minimum mathematics proficiency.
- The report identifies segregation and unequal educational outcomes as challenges to inclusion.

Useful direct quotes:
> "School systems mirror the highly unequal societies in which they are situated."

> "One in two Latin American 15-year-olds does not achieve minimum proficiency in reading."

Implication: This validates a broad learning-access and inclusion problem in the region, but it does not by itself prove that Island Tales is the preferred solution. It supports testing whether culturally relevant, accessible digital stories can complement—not replace—school literacy supports.

## Research discipline
These sources are evidence about problems and context, not proof of product-market fit. Direct customer interviews, surveys, pilot usage, or paid commitments are still required before making a strong demand claim.

## Source 3 — OECS / USAID Early Learners Programme
URL: https://pressroom.oecs.int/oecs--usaid-early-learners-programme-advances-culturally-relevant-content

Evidence type: Official regional education institution announcement.

Key findings:
- The OECS/USAID Early Learners Programme explicitly recognizes a "tremendous shortage" of culturally relevant books in schools across the OECS.
- The programme created locally authored early-grade books and distributed them in print and electronic formats to OECS member-state schools.
- The announcement reports 43 entrants' books being published and almost 51,000 locally/regionally authored books being printed and distributed.
- The programme links engaging, culturally relevant reading resources to student motivation and interest in reading.
- The initiative involved children, parents, teachers, and other community members in creating or co-creating books, which is a strong signal that the problem is institutionally recognized and that local content can be adopted at meaningful scale.

Useful direct quote:
> "The ELP recognizes the tremendous shortage of culturally relevant books in schools across the OECS."

> "The printing and distribution of almost 51,000 locally/regionally authored books is a significant undertaking."

Implication: This is the strongest evidence so far of school/institutional demand and actual adoption of Caribbean-authored literacy content. It validates the problem and the content category, but it does not prove demand for Island Tales specifically; a pilot should test whether digital discovery, narration, and family engagement add value beyond printed books.

## Source 4 — Small Axe Project interview with Anthony Williams, Caribbean Book Blog creator
URL: https://smallaxe.net/sxsalon/interviews/interview-anthony-williams-creator-caribbean-book-blog

Evidence type: First-person interview with a Caribbean media/platform creator; historical source (April 2011).

Key findings:
- Williams says Caribbean writers, especially region-based writers, struggle to get publishers' attention, while publishers often claim demand is insufficient.
- He reports that his own interviews and review of online message boards indicated untapped demand and readers complaining that Caribbean books were difficult to find in the region and metropolitan markets.
- He identifies dispersed diaspora audiences and few bookstores selling Caribbean books as structural distribution problems.
- He argues writers need new media and online outlets to showcase work and build attention.

Useful direct quotes:
> "Publishers claim that there is not enough demand for Caribbean literature to make it profitable."

> "There is a demand for Caribbean books that is not being tapped, and ... readers are complaining that it is difficult to find good books by Caribbean writers."

> "They need more publishing opportunities and other avenues to showcase their work."

Implication: This directly supports testing discoverability and audience-building as the core job-to-be-done for authors and readers. Because the interview is old, use it as qualitative historical evidence and seek current author validation before treating it as current market proof.

## Source 5 — Tucker, Murphy & Chalmers, *It Starts at Home: Home Language and Literacy Practices in Jamaica*
URL: https://caribed.scholasticahq.com/article/143662-it-starts-at-home-home-language-and-literacy-practices-in-jamaica
DOI: https://doi.org/10.46425/cjed701021215
Full text downloaded for local review: `/home/ubuntu/Island-Tales/tucker_jamaica_home_literacy.txt`

Evidence type: Peer-reviewed Caribbean education study (Caribbean Journal of Education and Development, 2025; issue identified as Vol. 1, Issue 2, 2024).

Key findings from the full text:
- The study examines the home language and literacy environments of Jamaican primary-school learners.
- Parents most commonly reported writing or typing (61%), playing word games (41%), and reading non-work-related materials such as books (36%) either often or very often while being observed by their children.
- More formal direct literacy practices were less common, including shared book reading involving explicit attention to letters and words and creative writing activities with children.
- More than 75% of respondents strongly agreed that parents can help at home and should encourage interest and confidence in reading and writing, but parents were less supportive of putting the main instructional responsibility on themselves; comments emphasized teachers as the primary instructors.
- The study notes that many children were raised by older caregivers, that Jamaican Creole and English both shaped the home language environment, and that some caregivers faced limited English literacy or other constraints.
- The paper calls for context-sensitive community literacy practices and accessible resources for caregivers, while noting that the study design does not prove which digital product would solve the problem.

Useful direct quote from a participant:
> "Teachers are there to teach, whilst parents are to reinforce what was taught."

Implication: Family demand is evidenced as a need for practical, culturally and linguistically sensitive support that makes home reading easier; it is not evidence that every family wants a standalone app. Island Tales should therefore test caregiver usability, language fit, offline/low-bandwidth access, and whether parents value narration and guided prompts that complement teachers rather than replace them.

## Source 6 — OECS Commission Global Digital Library announcement
URL: https://pressroom.oecs.int/oecs-commission-launches-global-digital-library

Evidence type: Official OECS institutional announcement.

Key findings:
- The Global Digital Library is an open-education repository targeting early learners, with more than 6,000 books in 83 languages and a growing early-mathematics repository.
- It is designed for children, teachers, parents, and other caretakers.
- The OECS planned to integrate the library and other OECS content into its e-learning platform and described a broader K-12 digital ecosystem.
- The announcement explicitly says that online learning does not automatically widen access and that digital resources must address cost, geography, time, and entry barriers.

Useful direct quote:
> "Covid-19 has taught us that pivoting to online learning has not led to an automatic widening of access and participation."

Implication: This establishes a strong existing alternative and a constraint: Island Tales cannot win merely by being digital. Its differentiation must be Caribbean-specific discovery, quality curation, engaging interaction, author visibility, and a delivery model that works under real access constraints.

## Source 7 — OECS/USAID Early Learners Programme video
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DA0biYsCITw
Saved analysis: `/home/ubuntu/Island-Tales/video_DA0biYsCITw_analysis_20260907_053736.md`

Evidence type: First-hand video statements from principals and educational leaders across Antigua, Dominica, St. Vincent, St. Kitts & Nevis, St. Lucia, and Grenada. The video is programme-produced, so it is a stakeholder perspective rather than an independent evaluation.

Key findings:
- Principals describe a shift toward balanced and integrated reading instruction.
- They emphasize variety of books, daily reading, independent reading time, local authors, culturally relevant books, and teacher collaboration across OECS states.
- One speaker links the programme to online learning opportunities, indicating some institutional readiness for digital delivery.
- The video contains no independent outcome metrics and does not directly establish parent demand or willingness to pay.

Useful direct quotes from the video analysis:
> "Teachers are using a variety of books in the classroom, which is so important for children to find content that they are interested in." — Ms. Annita Peter, Principal, St Luke’s Primary School, Dominica.

> "We have a community of local authors in the OECS producing culturally relevant books that can be shared with our students." — Mrs. Christine Merville, Principal, Castries Anglican Infant School, St. Lucia.

> "Early grade teachers are collaborating and sharing information to improve reading in the OECS." — Jacqueline Noel, Principal, St George’s SDA Primary School, Grenada.

Implication: This supports the school-side jobs of finding engaging variety, using culturally relevant content, and sharing resources across institutions. It strengthens the case for a teacher-facing discovery and curation layer, but must not be presented as proof of product-market fit or learning impact.

## Source 8 — CaribbeanReads catalogue
URL: https://www.caribbeanreads.com/books/

Evidence type: Current commercial/curated Caribbean books catalogue and distribution alternative.

Key findings:
- The site provides navigation for books, authors, illustrators, bulk orders, distributors, a downloadable catalogue, books by country, and reading guides.
- The catalogue visibly includes many children's and youth titles, Caribbean settings, country-specific stories, activity books, and at least some Spanish-language titles.
- It is a meaningful existing alternative for discovery and purchase, especially for readers, schools, and bulk buyers.
- The catalogue page is primarily a static discovery and commerce experience; it does not visibly provide Island Tales' proposed combination of narrated stories, comprehension questions, reading-history personalization, badges, and interactive choice-based reading.

Implication: The competitive gap is not "Caribbean books do not exist." The validated opportunity is more precise: improve **discovery, engagement, guided family/school use, and author reach** around existing and new Caribbean children's content, while differentiating from book catalogues and institutional repositories.
