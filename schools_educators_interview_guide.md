# Schools & Educators Customer Discovery Interview Guide

**Project:** Caribbean Scribbles ecosystem / Island Tales  
**Audience:** Teachers, literacy leads, librarians, principals, curriculum coordinators, and other educators involved in selecting or using children's learning resources  
**Estimated interview time:** 30–45 minutes  
**Purpose:** Understand how schools and educators currently find, evaluate, adopt, use, and fund children's literacy and cultural resources.

## Research principles

This guide is designed to discover educators' actual needs without telling them that they lack Caribbean resources, need more digital content, or have a particular classroom problem. Begin with a recent real event. Ask the participant to describe what happened, what they used, what they rejected, and what the result was. Only introduce the Caribbean Scribbles concept after the participant's current workflow and alternatives are understood.

Do not treat positive comments about a product idea as evidence of demand. Stronger evidence includes repeated use in a real class, a teacher sharing the resource with a colleague or family, a request for a pilot, a named decision-maker, a procurement pathway, or a budget discussion.

# Part 1 — Screening script

## Opening message for recruitment

> Hello, I’m speaking with educators and school staff to understand how schools currently find and use children’s books, stories, literacy materials, and cultural resources. We are not selling anything, and there are no right or wrong answers. We are interested in what you actually did recently, including resources that worked well, resources you rejected, and situations where you had to improvise. The conversation should take approximately 30–45 minutes. Would you be willing to answer a few screening questions?

## Screening questions

| Question | Qualifying response | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Which role best describes your current work? | Teacher, literacy lead, librarian, principal, curriculum coordinator, special-education/support educator, teaching assistant, or relevant school staff. | Record current role and decision involvement. |
| 2. Do you currently work with children or educators in approximately the 3–12 age range? | Yes | Record grade band or age range. |
| 3. In the past three months, have you personally selected, adapted, recommended, purchased, borrowed, downloaded, or used a book, story, reading activity, or cultural resource with learners? | Yes | This confirms recent behavior. |
| 4. Can you describe one recent example? | Can describe a specific resource, context, decision, and result. | If no specific example, classify as lower priority for the first round. |
| 5. Were you involved in deciding whether the resource would be used? | Yes, fully or partly | Classify as user, recommender, approver, purchaser, or influencer. |
| 6. Are you willing to discuss resources that worked and did not work? | Yes | Helps avoid only positive feedback. |
| 7. Are you comfortable discussing the school or programme without naming individual children? | Yes | Do not collect student names or identifying information. |
| 8. Are you directly employed by Caribbean Scribbles, involved in this research, or already helping design the product? | No preferred | If yes, record separately as an informed stakeholder. |

## Optional sampling questions

Ask only what is needed to build a balanced sample:

1. Which country, territory, or community is the school or programme located in?
2. Is the setting public, private, community-based, faith-based, NGO-led, or another type?
3. Which grades or age groups do you serve?
4. Approximately how many learners are in the relevant class, grade, or programme? Use a broad range rather than exact numbers.
5. What types of access are normally available: classroom print materials, school library, smartphone, tablet, computer, projector, Wi-Fi, mobile data, or offline resources?
6. Are you involved in recommending, approving, purchasing, funding, or implementing learning resources?

Do not use these questions to assume that one school type, territory, language, or technology level is better. The purpose is to compare different operating contexts.

## Screening close

> Thank you. Based on your answers, we would like to invite you to a 30–45 minute conversation. We will focus on a recent resource-selection or classroom-use experience rather than asking you to predict what you might do in the future. You may skip any question or stop at any time. Are you comfortable continuing or scheduling the interview?

# Part 2 — Interview introduction and consent

## Interviewer introduction

> Thank you for speaking with me. I’m researching how educators and schools currently find, evaluate, and use children's books, stories, literacy activities, and cultural resources. I’m trying to understand the current workflow as it really happens. I am not looking for a particular answer, and criticism is just as useful as positive feedback.

> I’ll ask about a recent example first. Later, I may briefly show or describe a possible resource and ask for your reaction. The early part of the conversation is about your current experience, so there is no product to evaluate yet.

## Consent checklist

Confirm that the participant understands:

- The purpose and approximate duration of the interview.
- Whether audio or video recording will be used.
- That participation is voluntary.
- That they may skip questions or stop at any time.
- That no student names, images, voices, or identifying records are required.
- How notes and recordings will be stored and who may review them.
- Whether anonymized quotations may be used internally or in a report.
- Whether the participant may be contacted for a follow-up prototype or pilot.

Suggested consent question:

> Do you understand the purpose of the conversation and agree to participate under these conditions?

# Part 3 — Core interview guide

## Section A — Role and working context

1. Tell me about your current role and the learners or educators you work with.
2. What kinds of reading, language, cultural, or learning activities are part of your work?
3. How are resources normally selected or introduced in your setting?
4. Who else is involved when a new resource is considered?
5. What constraints or requirements shape that decision?

**Neutral probes:**

- “Can you give me an example from this term?”
- “Who made the final decision?”
- “What happened after the resource was approved?”
- “What would a colleague say about that process?”

Avoid saying: “Schools need more Caribbean resources, don’t they?”

---

## Section B — Recent resource-selection walkthrough

> Think about the most recent time you needed or chose a book, story, reading activity, teaching resource, or cultural resource for learners. Please walk me through what happened from the beginning.

Ask in sequence:

1. What prompted the search or decision?
2. Where did you look first?
3. Who else did you consult?
4. What options did you find?
5. How did you compare them?
6. What criteria mattered most?
7. Which option did you choose, adapt, or reject?
8. How much preparation was required?
9. Was approval, funding, licensing, printing, training, or technical support needed?
10. How did learners respond?
11. How did you decide whether the resource worked?
12. What happened after the first use?

**Fact-finding probes:**

- “What was the name or type of resource, if you remember?”
- “How long did the search take?”
- “What did the resource cost, if anything?”
- “What made you reject the other option?”
- “Did you use it again?”
- “Did anyone else receive or use the resource?”

If the participant says the process was easy, ask what made it easy and whether that condition is normally available.

---

## Section C — Current resources and alternatives

1. What resources do you currently use most often for reading or cultural learning?
2. Which sources do you trust when choosing a new resource?
3. What works particularly well about the resources you use now?
4. What requires adaptation or extra preparation?
5. Have you ever created, translated, printed, or adapted material yourself? Tell me about the last time.
6. Have you ever stopped using a resource? What happened?
7. When a preferred resource is unavailable, what do you use instead?
8. What do colleagues, families, or school leaders usually recommend?
9. How do school libraries, public libraries, ministries, NGOs, publishers, or community groups contribute to your resource supply?

**Important probes:**

- “What did you actually do the last time?”
- “How often does that occur?”
- “What is the consequence when the resource is unavailable?”
- “What makes the workaround acceptable or unacceptable?”

Do not assume that a school has a shortage. Let the participant describe supply, access, quality, and workflow in their own terms.

---

## Section D — Learner fit and educational use

1. How do you decide whether a story or resource is appropriate for your learners?
2. What makes learners interested enough to continue?
3. What makes them lose interest or stop participating?
4. How do you adapt a resource for different reading levels, learning needs, or languages?
5. What happens when learners respond differently from what you expected?
6. How do families interact with the resources used at school?
7. What evidence tells you that a resource helped, engaged, or supported learners?
8. Can you recall a resource that learners discussed, shared, or requested again? What made it work?

If the participant independently raises Caribbean identity, local culture, language, or representation, ask:

- “Can you tell me about a recent example?”
- “What did learners or families do?”
- “How did you decide the resource was a good fit?”
- “What would you have used if that resource were unavailable?”

Avoid asking: “Would culturally authentic stories improve engagement?”

---

## Section E — Curriculum, planning, and workload

1. How does a new resource fit into your existing curriculum or teaching plan?
2. What preparation is usually required before learners can use it?
3. Which parts of preparation take the most time?
4. What types of guidance or supporting material do educators actually use?
5. How do you share resources with colleagues or families?
6. What training or technical support is normally available?
7. What causes a resource to be used once but not again?
8. How do you document or report resource use, if at all?

These questions distinguish a resource that educators like from one that fits a real workflow.

---

## Section F — Access, technology, and implementation

Do not assume that digital delivery is automatically better.

1. Where do learners normally access reading or story resources?
2. What devices, printed materials, connectivity, or school facilities are available?
3. What happens when the device, internet connection, data plan, electricity, or quiet space is unavailable?
4. Which resources work offline or with limited connectivity?
5. How do you handle passwords, logins, downloads, printing, or sharing?
6. What technology-related problems have affected a recent learning activity?
7. When is a digital resource more useful than a printed resource, and when is it less useful?
8. What would make a resource easy or difficult for another teacher to adopt?

Ask for examples instead of offering solutions:

- “Tell me about the last time that happened.”
- “What did you do next?”
- “How often does that situation occur?”

---

## Section G — Decision-making, procurement, and budget

1. Who can recommend a new resource?
2. Who approves it?
3. Who pays for it or identifies funding?
4. What is the usual process for buying, licensing, borrowing, or receiving resources?
5. What evidence is required before approval?
6. What budget category or funding source would normally apply?
7. What makes a resource too difficult to procure or sustain?
8. Have you recently paid for, licensed, or requested a resource? What happened?
9. What happens when a resource is free but requires staff time, devices, training, or data?
10. Which organisations or partners influence resource adoption?

Do not ask, “Would your school pay for Island Tales?” before understanding the actual procurement route. A teacher may be a strong user but not the buyer.

# Part 4 — Optional concept test

Use only after Sections A–G are complete.

## Neutral concept description

> Caribbean Scribbles is exploring a possible set of Caribbean children's literacy and cultural resources for schools and educators. It could include books, activities, reading programmes, and optional digital story experiences. The final format and delivery model are not decided. We are testing whether any part of this would be useful in real school or programme settings.

Ask:

1. What is your first reaction to this description?
2. Which part, if any, connects to something you described earlier?
3. Which part seems unnecessary or difficult to use?
4. What would you need to know before recommending or adopting it?
5. What would make it fit—or not fit—your current curriculum and workload?
6. What would stop the school or programme from trying it?
7. Who else would need to approve or be involved?
8. What would be the next concrete step you would be willing to take?

If the participant says they would use it, follow with:

> What would you be willing to do next: review one resource, try it with a class, share it with a colleague, introduce a decision-maker, join a pilot, or discuss a procurement route?

Do not defend the concept. Ask what should be removed before asking what should be added.

## Behavioral task options

Choose one task appropriate to the prototype stage:

- Find a resource for a specified age group and explain the selection criteria.
- Decide whether a story fits a real lesson or reading routine.
- Use a short story or activity with learners and record what happened.
- Share a resource with a colleague or family and observe whether they can access it.
- Identify what would be required to approve, procure, license, or distribute the resource.
- Draft a realistic pilot plan for one class, grade, library, or programme.

Measure completion, repeat use, sharing, and concrete next steps—not enthusiasm alone.

## Concept-test close

> What would make this worth using again? What would make you stop using it? Who should Caribbean Scribbles speak with next to understand this decision properly?

# Part 5 — Interviewer watch-outs

## Leading questions to avoid

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| “Do you need more Caribbean books?” | “How do you decide which books or stories to use?” |
| “Is it difficult to find culturally relevant resources?” | “Tell me about the last time you searched for a particular type of resource.” |
| “Would digital stories save teachers time?” | “How much preparation did the last resource require?” |
| “Would you use a Caribbean story platform?” | “What do you use now, and what would make you change?” |
| “Would your school pay for this?” | “How are comparable resources approved and funded?” |
| “Would teacher guides help?” | “What guidance do you currently use before trying a new resource?” |
| “Would students be more engaged with local stories?” | “Tell me about the last resource that learners continued using or requested again.” |
| “Would a pilot be useful?” | “What would need to happen before your school could try something new?” |

## Common interviewer errors

Do not treat a teacher's personal enthusiasm as school adoption. Do not confuse a principal's support with an approved budget. Do not ask educators to design the entire product before establishing the problem. Do not assume a resource is suitable because it is Caribbean-authored. Do not gather student names, images, assessment records, or identifiable classroom information. Do not imply that a school is underperforming because it uses existing resources.

# Part 7 — Evidence thresholds for the Schools & Educators segment

Use these as decision rules, not as fabricated results.

| Signal | Suggested first-round threshold |
|---|---:|
| Educators describing a recent resource-selection or classroom-use event | At least 6 of 8 participants |
| Participants describing a recurring cost, constraint, or consequence in the current workflow | At least 5 of 8 |
| Participants identifying a specific use case that a new resource might improve | At least 5 of 8 |
| Participants completing a prototype or classroom-fit task | At least 5 of 8 |
| Participants using or sharing a resource in a real setting | At least 3 of 8 |
| Participants introducing a decision-maker, partner, or procurement contact | At least 2 of 8 |
| Schools or educators agreeing to a defined pilot or procurement discussion | At least 2 of 8 |

A strong school-side signal requires more than positive feedback. Look for repeated use, colleague referrals, a named approval path, a real classroom trial, or a defined institutional next step.

## Safeguarding and institutional permissions

Obtain individual consent from adult participants. Obtain school or programme permission before observing a class or distributing materials to learners. Do not collect student names, photographs, voice recordings, identifiable work, assessment records, or health information unless a separate approved safeguarding process exists. If children participate in a prototype activity, secure the appropriate caregiver and institutional permissions first.
