# Parents & Families Customer Discovery Interview Guide

**Project:** Caribbean Scribbles ecosystem / Island Tales  
**Audience:** Parents, grandparents, guardians, and other caregivers of children approximately ages 3–12  
**Purpose:** Understand how families currently choose, access, and use children's books, stories, literacy activities, and cultural resources. This guide is designed to discover participants' actual experiences without telling them what their problems are.

## Research principles

The interviewer should ask about **recent behavior**, not hypothetical opinions. Begin with a real event, understand the current workaround, and explore the consequences before describing Caribbean Scribbles or Island Tales. Do not begin by asking whether the participant wants culturally relevant books, digital stories, narration, educational activities, or a new app. Those are possible solutions and must not be treated as established needs.

Use the participant's language. Do not correct their description of reading, storytelling, learning, culture, or family routines. Ask permission before recording. Avoid collecting unnecessary information about children, including names, school details, photographs, or identifiable reading records.

---

# Part 1 — Screening script

## Opening message for recruitment

> Hello, I’m speaking with caregivers to understand how families choose and use books, stories, and activities for children. We are not selling anything, and there are no right or wrong answers. We are interested in what you actually did recently, including what worked, what did not work, and what you used instead. The conversation should take approximately 25–35 minutes. We will not need your child’s name or any identifying information. Would you be willing to answer a few screening questions?

## Screening questions

Ask these questions before scheduling the full interview. Keep the wording neutral and do not explain the hypothesis being tested.

| Question | Qualifying response | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Do you currently care for or regularly spend time reading, telling stories, or choosing activities for at least one child between approximately 3 and 12 years old? | Yes | Record age range only, not the child’s name. |
| 2. In the past three months, have you personally chosen, found, bought, borrowed, downloaded, or shared a book, story, reading activity, puzzle, or educational resource for a child? | Yes | This confirms recent behavior rather than general interest. |
| 3. Can you describe one recent example of doing that? | Can describe a specific event, source, decision, and outcome. | If the participant cannot recall a recent example, do not treat them as a primary interview candidate. |
| 4. Were you personally involved in deciding what the child would use? | Yes, fully or partly | Record whether the participant was a user, recommender, purchaser, or gatekeeper. |
| 5. Are you comfortable discussing the process without sharing your child’s name, school, image, voice, or other identifying details? | Yes | Required for participation. |
| 6. Have you worked for Caribbean Scribbles Publishing, helped conduct this research, or participated in a previous interview about the same project? | No preferred | If yes, record separately as a potentially informed participant. |
| 7. Are you willing to discuss what you actually did, including options you rejected or stopped using? | Yes | Helps avoid purely aspirational feedback. |

## Optional diversity questions

Ask only what is needed for sampling. These questions are for comparison, not for judging eligibility.

1. Which country, territory, or community do you most identify with in relation to your family or cultural background?
2. Where does the child usually read or listen to stories: home, school, library, religious setting, community programme, or somewhere else?
3. What type of access is normally available during reading time: printed books, smartphone, tablet, computer, television, mobile data, Wi-Fi, or another arrangement?
4. Which caregiver role best describes you: parent, grandparent, guardian, older sibling, teacher-caregiver, or another role?
5. What age range is the child or children you usually support?

Do not use these questions to imply that one country, language, device, or caregiver arrangement is preferable. Record them only to ensure the research does not represent one narrow family context.

## Screening close

> Thank you. Based on your answers, we would like to invite you to a 25–35 minute conversation. The discussion will focus on a recent experience rather than asking you to predict what you might do in the future. You can skip any question, and you can stop at any time. Are you comfortable continuing or scheduling the interview?

# Part 2 — Interview introduction and consent

## Interviewer introduction

> Thank you for speaking with me. I’m researching how families currently find and use stories, books, and activities for children. I’m trying to understand the experience as it exists today. I am not testing you, and I am not looking for a particular answer. Please feel free to describe something that worked well, something that was frustrating, or a situation where you decided not to use anything.

> I will ask about a recent example first. Later, I may briefly show or describe a possible resource and ask for your reaction. The early part of the conversation is about your experience, so there are no product ideas to evaluate yet.

## Consent checklist

Confirm verbally or in writing that the participant understands:

- The purpose of the conversation.
- The expected duration.
- Whether audio recording will be used.
- That participation is voluntary.
- That they may skip questions or stop at any time.
- That the research will not require the child’s name or identifying information.
- How notes or recordings will be stored and who will see them.
- Whether anonymized quotes may be used in internal research.

Suggested consent question:

> Do you understand the purpose of the conversation and agree to participate under these conditions?

# Part 3 — Core interview guide

## Section A — Warm-up and family context

Use only enough questions to understand the participant's context. Do not turn this into a demographic survey.

1. Tell me a little about the kinds of books, stories, games, or activities children in your household usually enjoy.
2. When during a normal week do children most often read, listen to stories, or do learning activities?
3. Who usually chooses what they use?
4. What usually influences that choice?

**Neutral probes:**

- “Can you give me an example?”
- “What happened the last time?”
- “Who else was involved?”
- “How did the child respond?”
- “What did you do next?”

Avoid saying: “So you need more Caribbean books, right?”

---

## Section B — Recent-event walkthrough

This is the most important part of the interview. Ask the participant to reconstruct one recent event step by step.

> Think about the most recent time you chose, found, bought, borrowed, downloaded, or shared a story, book, puzzle, or educational activity for a child. Please walk me through what happened from the beginning.

Follow-up sequence:

1. What prompted you to look for something?
2. Where did you look first?
3. What other places or people did you consider?
4. What options did you find?
5. How did you compare the options?
6. What did you eventually choose—or why did you choose nothing?
7. How much time did the process take?
8. Did it cost money, data, travel, delivery, printing, or another resource?
9. What happened when the child used it?
10. What did you do afterward?

**Probe for facts:**

- “What was the title or type of resource, if you remember?”
- “Where did you get it?”
- “Was that your first choice?”
- “What made you reject the other option?”
- “How often does a situation like this occur?”

Do not suggest that the participant experienced difficulty. If they say the process was easy, explore what made it easy and whether that is consistently true.

---

## Section C — Current alternatives and workarounds

1. What do you usually use when you want a child to read, listen to a story, practise a skill, or learn about a place or culture?
2. Which option do you use most often?
3. What works well about it?
4. What does not work well?
5. Have you ever stopped using or abandoned an option? What happened?
6. When the preferred option is unavailable, what do you do instead?
7. Who do you ask for recommendations?
8. Have you ever created, adapted, translated, printed, or retold something yourself?

**Important probes:**

- “What did you do the last time?”
- “How often do you use that workaround?”
- “What is the hardest part of the workaround?”
- “What would happen if that option were no longer available?”

These questions reveal the participant's real alternatives and the strength of the current habit. A participant who has no workaround or no recurring situation may not have a strong unmet need.

---

## Section D — Fit, relevance, and enjoyment

Explore what makes content feel appropriate without implying that culture is absent or that recognition is automatically important.

1. How do you decide whether a story or activity is a good fit for a child?
2. What makes a child interested enough to continue?
3. What makes a child lose interest?
4. Are there topics, settings, characters, languages, illustrations, or types of humor that work particularly well or poorly for your family?
5. Can you recall a story or activity that felt especially meaningful or memorable? What made it so?
6. Are there stories, traditions, places, or experiences you would like children to encounter more often? Tell me about the last time you looked for one.
7. How do family members influence what children read or listen to?
8. How do school, community, religious, or cultural activities influence those choices?

Avoid asking: “Do you want more culturally authentic books?”

If the participant independently raises Caribbean identity, representation, language, heritage, or cultural authenticity, ask:

- “Can you tell me about a recent example?”
- “What did you choose?”
- “What difference did that make for the child?”
- “What did you do when you could not find what you wanted?”

---

## Section E — Access and practical constraints

Do not assume that digital access, print access, or connectivity is the problem. Ask how the participant actually manages access.

1. Where does the child usually access stories or reading activities?
2. What devices, printed materials, or community resources are available?
3. What happens when the preferred device, internet connection, data plan, book, or quiet space is unavailable?
4. Are there times when downloading, streaming, printing, or travelling is inconvenient?
5. What makes it easy or difficult for an adult and child to use something together?
6. Does the adult usually stay involved, or does the child use the resource independently?
7. What would make a resource easier to use in your actual home routine?

If the participant identifies a constraint, ask for a recent example rather than offering a solution.

---

## Section F — Decision-making and value

1. Who decides whether a new book, story, activity, or learning resource is used?
2. Who pays for it, if anyone?
3. What kinds of resources have you paid for recently?
4. What makes something worth paying for?
5. What makes you decide not to pay?
6. What recommendations or evidence do you trust before trying something new?
7. Have you ever recommended or shared a children's resource with another family? What prompted you to do so?

Do not ask for a hypothetical price too early. First understand actual spending and the participant's current alternatives.

# Part 4 — Optional concept test

Only use this section after Sections A–F are complete. The interviewer should not imply that the concept solves a problem the participant has already confirmed.

## Neutral concept description

> We are exploring a possible Caribbean Scribbles service that could bring together Caribbean children's stories, books, activities, and optional digital experiences. Depending on the final design, it might help families discover content, use stories together, and find related activities. We are still testing whether this would be useful and which parts, if any, matter to families.

Ask:

1. What is your first reaction?
2. Which part, if any, connects to something you described earlier?
3. Which part seems unnecessary or less useful?
4. What would you expect to see before trusting it?
5. What might stop you from trying it?
6. How would you compare it with what you use now?
7. Who else would need to be involved before your household used it?
8. What would you do next if you wanted to try it?

Do not ask: “Would you use it?” as the main measure. If the participant says yes, follow with:

> What would be the next concrete step you would be willing to take—try a story, share it, recommend it, join a short pilot, or something else?

## Behavioral task options

Choose one task appropriate to the prototype stage:

- Find a story for a child of a specified age and explain why it was selected.
- Start a story and use the narration or read-aloud option if available.
- Decide whether the story is appropriate to share with a child.
- Find a related activity or discussion prompt.
- Share the story with another caregiver.
- Return one week later and choose another story.

Record what the participant does, not only what they say. Note where they hesitate, what they ignore, and whether they complete the task without coaching.

## Concept-test close

> What, if anything, would make this worth returning to? What would make you stop using it? Is there anyone else you think should be involved in deciding whether it is used?

Thank the participant without defending the concept or correcting their criticism.

# Part 5 — Interviewer watch-outs

## Leading questions to avoid

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| “Do you need more culturally relevant books?” | “How do you decide whether a story is a good fit?” |
| “Would narrated stories help your child read?” | “Tell me about the last time a child listened to or read a story. What happened?” |
| “Is it hard to find Caribbean books?” | “Where did you look the last time you wanted a particular kind of story?” |
| “Would you use an Island Tales app?” | “What do you use now, and what would you do next if it were unavailable?” |
| “Would you pay $X?” | “What have you paid for recently, and what made it worth paying for?” |
| “Do you want more activities and puzzles?” | “What activities does the child use now, and how are they chosen?” |
| “Would this save you time?” | “How much time did the last search or preparation take?” |

## Common interviewer errors

Do not fill silences too quickly, defend the product, reward positive answers, or convert a participant's general preference into a confirmed problem. Do not ask about five product features in one question. Do not interview only people who already know Caribbean Scribbles. Do not treat a participant's willingness to be helpful as evidence that the product is valuable.

# Part 7 — Evidence threshold for the Parents & Families segment

Do not conclude that the segment has strong demand based only on favorable opinions. Treat the segment as promising when several independent participants can describe a recent recurring situation, a meaningful consequence, and a current workaround, and when participants complete a relevant task without heavy coaching.

A practical first-round threshold is:

| Signal | Suggested threshold |
|---|---:|
| Participants describing a recent, recurring resource-selection or shared-reading situation | At least 6 of 8 interviewed caregivers |
| Participants identifying a meaningful cost, frustration, or missed outcome in the current approach | At least 5 of 8 |
| Participants who complete a neutral prototype task | At least 5 of 8 |
| Participants who return, share, or recommend after the first task without being prompted | At least 3 of 8 |
| Participants willing to join a defined pilot or discuss a real purchase | At least 2 of 8 |

These are decision thresholds, not fabricated results. Replace them with actual counts after interviews are completed.
